Investing Is a Skill — Not a Talent

By Uncapped Investing · ~13 min read · July 2026

Most people treat investing like luck — like gambling. And because they think it's just luck, they never actually bother to learn it. That belief is comfortable, and it's wrong. Investing is a skill — just like football, just like chess. Something you can learn and get better at, step by step. Once you accept that, everything changes: your expectations, your patience, and the way you measure whether you're actually getting better. This is the long version of the idea — the one I couldn't fully fit into a video.

And here's something I really believe: you learn a skill best with other people — people who are just as obsessed and curious as you are. The fastest way to get good at anything is to study the best, borrow their strategy, walk through it yourself — and then, once you're good enough, use all of it to build your own. That's the whole point of this channel.

Every skill needs patience first

Before any of the tactics, there's one thing every skill demands: patience. And the first step is admitting something uncomfortable — at the start, you have no idea what you're doing. There's a clean model for exactly this: the four stages of learning. You'll recognize yourself in every one. Let me walk you through them the way I actually lived them — through GameStop.

The four stages of learning (my GameStop story)

Stage 1 — unconscious incompetence: "I've got this"

You don't know what you don't know. Picture early 2021. GameStop is all over Reddit and YouTube, the price is exploding, and you buy in. At first it keeps ripping — you're up 30, 50 percent — and you think: this is easy, why isn't everyone doing this? You feel like a genius. You have no idea that you have no idea. Then the hype turns, and the stock gets cut in half. Suddenly you're deep in the red — and you don't even understand why.

Stage 2 — conscious incompetence: "I know nothing"

The fear pushes you to actually learn for the first time. You start asking the real question: what actually makes a company good? You open the numbers — is revenue growing or shrinking, is it profitable, how much debt, what's this thing even worth? And it hits you: GameStop was a shrinking mall retailer; the price was a short squeeze and a crowd, not the business. This stage feels terrible. But read this twice: this is exactly where real learning starts. Most people who quit investing quit right here — they mistake the feeling of learning for the feeling of not being good enough.

Stage 3 — conscious competence: "I can do it, but it takes effort"

Now you've got a strategy — a checklist. Growing or dying, margins, debt, moat, management, a fair price. But you still have to consciously work through every step. It's slow. That's not a weakness; that's what competence looks like before it becomes automatic. Pilots use checklists for a reason. You can do it now — and you're not flying blind anymore.

Stage 4 — unconscious competence: "second nature"

The next hype stock shows up, the next "this only goes up," and within thirty seconds your gut says: that's a crowd, not a company. Skip. Next. That's not talent — that's the scars from GameStop turned into instinct. There's a perfect example. Charlie Munger once said he'd never actually seen his partner Warren Buffett sit down and run a discounted-cash-flow model — if a company isn't obviously a great deal, Buffett just moves on to the next idea.

"Warren talks about these discounted cash flows, but I've never seen him do one. If it isn't perfectly obvious that it's going to work out well, he goes on to the next idea." — Charlie Munger

Let that sink in. Arguably the best investor alive doesn't reach for a calculator. He looks, and if it isn't obviously good, he's out. That's not magic — that's eighty years of practice compressed into instinct. That's stage four. You don't get there by being gifted; you get there by reps.

What actually makes a company good

To get to stage four, you first have to understand what actually makes a company good — not the stock price, the business itself. A few questions do most of the work:

The four questions

  1. Value: what is the business really worth?
  2. Customers: what value does it bring them — do they love it, do they keep coming back?
  3. Strategy: what's the plan, and what is it actually trying to achieve?
  4. The numbers: and do the numbers actually match the strategy, the principles and the goals?

Because a company can say anything. The numbers are what show you whether it's real. That last check — do the words and the numbers line up? — is where a lot of stories quietly fall apart.

Where to start: four books

So if you actually want to build this skill, here's where I'd start — four books, ordered from the gentlest on-ramp to the toughest read. The best way to learn any skill is to copy the greats first, then build your own style on top.

  1. One Up on Wall Street — Peter Lynch. The easiest starting point — motivating, and it teaches you to invest in what you actually understand.
  2. The Dhandho Investor — Mohnish Pabrai. Short and story-driven — it drills margin of safety and low-risk thinking.
  3. Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements — Mary Buffett & David Clark. This one teaches you to actually read the numbers.
  4. The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham. Save the toughest for last: Buffett calls it the best book on investing ever written, and says Chapters 8 and 20 have been the bedrock of his whole approach.

If you're serious, start here. Learn how they learned — then expand it, adapt it, and make it your own.

The catch: you can't see your progress

Here's the thing that makes investing different from every other skill. Normally you see your progress. In football or chess, after a few weeks or months you feel it — you're getting better. Investing doesn't work like that. You can beat the market for a whole year — and underperform it the next. You can even beat it three years in a row, and it still doesn't prove you're good — it might just be luck. Michael Mauboussin makes this point in The Success Equation: investing has a huge luck component, and the feedback is slow, noisy and often misleading.

There's a famous line from Benjamin Graham that Warren Buffett quotes all the time:

In the short run, the market is a voting machine — but in the long run, it's a weighing machine.

Short term, it runs on hype and emotion; long term, it weighs what a business is actually worth. That's the whole problem for you as a learner: in the short term, the result lies to you. A beginner on a lucky streak feels like a master; a genuinely skilled investor in a bad year doubts everything. The scoreboard is broken over weeks and months — it only tells the truth over years.

So build your own feedback

If the market won't give you honest feedback in the short term, you have to build it yourself. It's simpler than it sounds. Before you buy — or sell — write down your thesis: your actual reasons.

The decision journal

Before you act, write down:

  1. Why am I buying? The actual thesis in plain language.
  2. What has to be true for this to work? (growth, margins, a product, a moat)
  3. What would prove me wrong? The specific evidence that would make me sell.

Then, months later, go back and read it. Not to check if you made money — to check if your thinking was any good. Did what you predicted actually happen? Were you right for the reason you thought, or did you just get lucky?

That's the only honest way to tell if you're improving in the short term — because the market itself only tells you straight over the long run. Judge the process, not the short-term result.

The honest reality check

Now I have to be honest with you. Most investors don't beat the market — a lot of them actually do worse. Over ten years, only about 15.7% of large US funds beat the S&P 500; over fifteen years, only about 10.5% (SPIVA / S&P Dow Jones Indices). And these are professionals, with whole teams behind them. So this really is a skill — most people never master it.

So here's my honest take. If you're serious — if you're willing to really dig in and treat it almost like a hobby — then go for it. I'm obsessed with this; I'm on it basically every day. But if you don't have the time or the drive, then for most people the smartest move is a broad ETF or index fund: many companies in one, your risk spread out. And that is completely fine. Just don't do it blindly either — get the basic knowledge first. You should always understand what you're actually buying.

Summary

Let's pull it together. One: investing is a skill, not luck — you go through the same four stages as any skill, and the hardest one, where you realize you know nothing, is exactly where real learning starts. Two: understand what actually makes a company good — its value, its customers, its strategy, and whether the numbers back it up — and learn from the greats first, then build your own style. Three: in the short term the market won't give you honest feedback — only over the long run does the truth come out — so judge your process, not your short-term result. And be honest with yourself: if you don't have the time or the drive, a broad ETF is the smart, sensible choice — just never invest blindly.
