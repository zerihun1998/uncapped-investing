Why Community Is Your Biggest Edge as an Investor (If You Build It Right)

By Uncapped Investing · ~12 min read · July 2026 · my personal opinion, not investment advice

The best investors in the world share one thing nobody talks about: the people around them. Buffett had Munger. Every serious fund runs on a network. Yet most of us try to learn investing completely alone, staring at a screen — which I think is the slowest possible way to get good. But I'm not going to sell you the naive version of this. Community can also quietly wreck your returns. Here's both sides, honestly, and exactly how I use it.

How you actually get good at any skill

Think about how anyone gets good at football, an instrument, or a language. You surround yourself with people obsessed with the same thing. You watch how they play, study their moves, copy what works and drop what doesn't. Investing is no different. Alone, you can seriously track a handful of sectors. In a group where one person lives tech, another health, another energy, you suddenly cover a whole market — and every thesis gets stress-tested before you risk a cent. You also hear about the small company with huge potential months before it hits anyone's radar.

There's a name for this in research: the wisdom of crowds. Under the right conditions, a group of diverse people is smarter than almost any single genius in it.

The honest catch: most groups actually lose

Here's what the "join my community" pitches never tell you. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean studied 166 investment clubs. The average club returned 14.1% a year while the market returned 17.9% — and roughly 60% underperformed the index, doing worse than even the average solo investor. The title of their paper says everything: "Too Many Cooks Spoil the Profits."

Why does this happen? Groupthink. Everyone starts agreeing, the loudest voice wins, disagreement feels awkward, and the group piles into the same handful of ideas. The wisdom of crowds flips into the madness of crowds. A room full of people who all think alike isn't smarter than one of them — it's dumber, and much more confident about it.

The evidence isn't a reason to avoid community. It's a warning about what kind of community you build.

When a crowd is actually smart

James Surowiecki identified four conditions for a "wise" crowd: diversity of opinion, independence of judgment, decentralization of knowledge, and effective aggregation. For investors, the two that matter most are diversity and independence. Diversity means different sectors, styles and risk appetites in the room. Independence means you form your own view before the group washes over you. Those investment clubs didn't fail because they were groups — they failed because they became one big correlated brain: same information, same opinions, same trades.

My honest, slightly contrarian take

I don't actually think an "echo chamber" is automatically bad. When you're learning a skill, being surrounded by people obsessed with the same thing is a gift — as long as you keep one thing switched on: your own critical judgment. Soak up everything. Watch how the best people in your circle think. But question all of it, and run it through a single filter: does this fit my style? The danger was never the community. It's switching off your own brain and copying blindly.

My 5 rules for keeping a community an edge

  1. Write your own thesis before you read the group's. Protect your independence.
  2. Reward the person who disagrees. The contrarian is the most valuable member.
  3. Build diversity on purpose — different sectors, different methods.
  4. Agree on a shared standard for what "a good company" even means, so the noise has a filter.
  5. More voices ≠ more truth. It just means more to filter — keep thinking for yourself.

What this community is really for

The point isn't for everyone to converge on the same stocks. It's the opposite. I want to show you my strategy and my philosophy so you can build your own. Everyone brings something, everyone learns from everyone, and we each walk toward our own destination — together. A community's job isn't to make you think like it. It's to help you become the investor only you can be.
